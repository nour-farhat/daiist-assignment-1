## Business / real-life framing

For my business framing, I decided to focus on a life or critical-illness insurance company that wants to reduce future coronary heart disease (CHD) claims. The idea is that when someone applies for an insurance policy, the model predicts their probability of developing CHD within the next 10 years. Based on this probability, the insurer can classify applicants into three different risk categories. Low-risk applicants receive a standard policy with no additional action, moderate-risk applicants are offered a light prevention plan with regular check-ups and reminders, costing the insurer €200 per person, and high-risk applicants are offered a more complete prevention programme, costing the insurer €1,500 per person.

The reason I decided to focus on prevention rather than increasing insurance premiums is because pricing based on this type of data is quite restricted. For example, in the EU, insurers cannot use sex to determine premiums. Health data is also considered sensitive information and is strictly regulated under GDPR. I also thought that focusing on prevention would make the business decision clearer, as the model can help determine which applicants should receive additional preventive care. The 10-year prediction also fits with the long-term nature of life and critical-illness insurance policies.

Since the dataset does not contain any information about costs, I had to make some assumptions for my business framing. I assumed that a CHD claim would cost the insurer €30,000, that the full prevention programme could prevent 30% of claims, and that the light prevention plan could prevent 10% of claims (All are illustrative assumptions that I used to evaluate the financial impact of the model).

For the data split, since the dataset does not contain dates and each row represents a different patient, I decided to use a random stratified 60/20/20 split for training, validation and testing. There was therefore no need to use a time-based split. I used stratification because only around 15% of participants developed CHD, and I wanted to make sure that this proportion remained approximately the same across the three datasets. The validation set was used to select the regularisation strength, learning rate and risk thresholds, while the test set was only used at the end to evaluate the final model.

For the evaluation metric, I decided not to focus on accuracy because I realised that it could be misleading for this dataset. Since only around 15% of participants developed CHD, a model that predicts that nobody will develop the disease would still achieve 84.8% accuracy, even though it would fail to identify any of the actual cases. In my business framing, the most costly mistake would be failing to identify someone who will develop CHD, since this could result in a €30,000 claim for the insurer. On the other hand, unnecessarily assigning someone to a prevention programme would cost at most €1,500. Since the model predicts probabilities that are then used to estimate expected costs, I wanted to make sure that these probabilities were as reliable as possible. This is why I decided to evaluate the models using log loss and calibration, rather than focusing only on accuracy.

Finally, I needed to determine the thresholds that would separate applicants into the three risk categories. Based on my cost assumptions, I calculated two thresholds. The first one was 6.7%, above which the light prevention plan becomes financially worthwhile, calculated as €200 / (0.10 × €30,000). The second threshold was 21.7%, above which the full prevention programme becomes more cost-effective than the light plan, calculated as €1,300 / (0.20 × €30,000). I also tested these thresholds on the validation set and found that the full prevention programme threshold that generated the lowest cost was 23.5%, which was quite close to the theoretical threshold of 21.7%.

## Data preparation & feature engineering

Before doing this, I split the dataset into training, validation and test sets using a stratified 60/20/20 split, to make sure that the proportion of patients who developed CHD remained approximately the same in each set. I also wanted to avoid data leakage, so all the values used to replace missing data or scale the features were calculated using only the training set.

For the missing values, I used the median for numerical variables such as glucose, cholesterol, BMI and heart rate, since some of these variables had extreme values that could affect the mean. For categorical variables such as education and blood pressure medication, I used the most common value. For cigarettes per day, I used the median of smokers, which was 20, because all the patients with missing values for this variable were smokers. One limitation of this method is that it creates artificial values in the dataset. For example, 225 patients in the training set received the same glucose value of 78. I then looked at how the different variables were related to the probability of developing CHD to see which features could be useful. Another feature I created was an interaction between age and systolic blood pressure, since high blood pressure seemed to have a stronger impact on older patients.

I also tested several other features, such as whether someone was a heavy smoker, obese, had high cholesterol or had glucose levels above the diabetes threshold. ## Modeling: three implementations, one model

For the modelling part, I decided to use logistic regression because my target variable, TenYearCHD, is binary, meaning that it can only take the values 0 or 1. Linear regression would not be appropriate in this case because it predicts continuous values and could give results below 0 or above 1, which would not make sense as probabilities. Logistic regression uses a sigmoid function to make sure that the predictions are always between 0 and 1. I also thought that this model was appropriate for my business framing because I needed probabilities to calculate the expected costs for the insurer, and logistic regression also allows us to understand which factors influence the risk of developing CHD.

I then implemented logistic regression using three different methods: scikit-learn, a manual implementation in PyTorch and a standard PyTorch implementation using nn.Module and Adam. To make sure that the comparison was fair, I used the same training, validation and test sets, the same 14 scaled features and the same loss function with L2 regularisation for all three models. The main difference was how each model was trained. Scikit-learn uses its own solver, while for the manual PyTorch implementation I had to define the weights, bias, sigmoid function and loss function myself, and then update the weights using gradient descent. For the standard PyTorch implementation, I used the built-in functions and the Adam optimiser.

Before training the models, I had to choose the regularisation strength and the learning rates. For scikit-learn, I tested C = 0.01, 0.1, 1 and 10 on the validation set. C = 0.01, the strongest regularisation, gave the lowest validation log loss (0.3936), while C = 10, which is close to no regularisation, gave 0.3946, so regularisation helped slightly. I then converted this value for PyTorch, using λ = 1 / (2 · n · C) = 0.0197, so that all three models would optimise exactly the same objective. For the manual PyTorch model I tested learning rates of 0.01, 0.1 and 1.0 and kept 1.0, and for the standard PyTorch model I tested 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1 and kept 0.1, choosing the value with the lowest validation log loss in each case. I trained both PyTorch models for 5,000 epochs. The learning rate made almost no difference: all values reached a validation log loss between 0.39365 and 0.39368. Both models converged to the same training loss of 0.38178, which shows they reached the same minimum

Once the models were trained, I evaluated them on the test set, which I had not used during training or validation. I compared them using ROC-AUC, log loss and Brier score, since I wanted to evaluate both their ability to distinguish between patients who would develop CHD and those who would not, and the reliability of their predicted probabilities. I also compared the models to a naive baseline that simply predicts the same CHD probability of 15.2% for everyone. The baseline had a log loss of 0.4264 and an AUC of 0.5, while all three logistic regression models achieved almost exactly the same results, with an AUC of around 0.733, a log loss of 0.3779 and a Brier score of 0.1143. This means that the models were able to distinguish between higher-risk and lower-risk patients better than the baseline. For example, an AUC of 0.733 means that if we randomly select one patient who developed CHD and one who did not, the model would give a higher predicted risk to the patient who developed CHD around 73% of the time.

What I found interesting was that the three implementations gave almost identical predictions, even though they were trained differently. The maximum difference in predicted probability compared with scikit-learn was 0.0005 for the manual PyTorch model and 0.0026 for the nn.Module version, and the coefficients were also almost the same. This makes sense because all three models were using the same data, features and regularisation, and logistic regression with L2 regularisation has a convex objective, meaning that the different optimisation methods should converge towards the same minimum. The small differences can be explained by the different numerical precision and optimisation methods used. I also tested what would happen without regularisation and found that the test log loss increased to 0.3834, compared to 0.3779 with regularisation. This showed me that regularisation was useful for improving the model's performance.

I also wanted to check whether the predicted probabilities were reliable, since my business problem depends on using these probabilities to calculate expected insurance costs. To do this, I looked at the calibration of the model. On the test set, the model predicted approximately 126.9 CHD cases in total, while the actual number was 129. The expected calibration error was also 0.021, which means that the predicted probabilities were on average around 2 percentage points away from the observed rates across the calibration groups. I therefore considered the calibration results quite encouraging, although they do not guarantee that every individual prediction is accurate.

Another interesting part was looking at the coefficients to understand which variables had the strongest influence on the predictions. I used odds ratios to interpret them and found that age was one of the most important factors, with an odds ratio of 1.31 for an increase of one standard deviation, followed by sex and the interaction between age and systolic blood pressure, both with an odds ratio of around 1.26. Cigarettes per day, systolic blood pressure and glucose also increased the predicted risk. This was consistent with what I had observed during the exploratory data analysis and helped me better understand how the model was making its predictions.

Finally, I applied the predictions to my insurance business framing to see whether using the model could actually reduce expected costs. I compared four different strategies: doing nothing, offering the light prevention plan to everyone, offering the full programme to everyone, and using the model to divide applicants into three risk categories. The results showed that doing nothing would cost approximately €4,564 per applicant, while offering the light plan to everyone would cost €4,307 and the full programme to everyone would cost €4,695. Using the model with the three risk categories gave the lowest estimated cost of €4,129 per applicant. This was based on the cost and prevention effectiveness assumptions I had made earlier, so the results would need to be tested with real insurance data.

When looking more closely at the three categories, around 20.9% of applicants were classified as low risk, 58.6% as moderate risk and 20.5% as high risk. The actual CHD rates in these groups were 3.4%, 12.7% and 34.5%, respectively, which showed that the model was separating patients into groups with different levels of risk. Using the 21.7% threshold for the full prevention programme, the model identified 60 out of the 129 patients who actually developed CHD, which represents a recall of 46.5%. However, 69 patients who developed CHD were not assigned to the full programme, although most of them were still included in the moderate-risk category and would therefore receive the light prevention plan.
